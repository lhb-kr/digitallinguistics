Lear, Lover, or Lunatic: Mapping Shakespeare for the Streaming Library

A reproducible typology that sorts the thirty-seven plays by the shape of their emotional flow and the architecture of their character networks — and translates each type into an audience and a viewing occasion.

Team Captain Underpants Hee-yeon Jeong · Hyeong-bin Lee · Chan-woo Eom · Ho-moon Na

Streaming catalogues are vast, but the vocabulary used to navigate them is thin. Genre labels collapse very different viewing experiences into the same bin, and recommendation engines optimise for what was watched rather than for how a story feels. This working paper proposes an alternative reference frame built on a fixed, well-understood canon: the plays of Shakespeare. We characterise each play along two measurable axes — an emotional profile, derived from a deep-learning sentiment arc, and a relational profile, derived from a quantified character network — and then combine them into eight named types, each anchored by a representative play. Every claim below is grounded in data exported from open corpora and re-rendered, from scratch, in the interactive figures embedded in this report. The intended end product is a typology general enough to be projected onto the wider film-and-series library.

§ 1 — Problem & Framing

Two axes, because two things move an audience

The brief asks for a classification that does not rest on intuition or impression. We therefore fixed, in advance, the two structural properties we believe most strongly govern how a viewer bonds with a narrative. The first is the movement of feeling over time: whether a story rises, falls, swings, or holds steady. The second is the shape of the relationships that carry that feeling: whether the emotional weight is borne by a single protagonist, two contending camps, or a distributed ensemble, and whether those figures inhabit one connected social world or several walled-off ones.

These two axes are deliberately orthogonal. A play can fall steeply through a tightly interconnected web (King Lear) or fall through isolated pockets of characters who barely meet; it can swing wildly inside a small enclosed cast (The Tempest) or swing across a sprawling political field. Treating emotion and structure as independent dimensions lets the same emotional shape resolve into different viewing experiences depending on the relational space it unfolds in — which is precisely the distinction a catalogue label usually loses.

The composite, stated once

Each play receives a coordinate (Sentiment Type, Social Field). The final typology is the product of these two axes, filtered down to the combinations that actually occur in the canon. Everything in §3–§5 is an elaboration of this single sentence.

§ 2 — Materials & Method

Two pipelines, one corpus

All measurements derive from two open resources. Play texts and structured speech data come from the DraCor Shakespeare corpus, which exposes each play as a network of speakers with co-occurrence weights and supports export to Gephi. Network metrics — degree, weighted degree, density, average path length, and modularity communities — were computed in Gephi. Sentiment arcs were produced by a deep-learning classifier and smoothed before export. Crucially, the visualisations in this report are not embeds of any external site: we exported the underlying numerical data and re-drew every chart and network from scratch, so the report is fully self-contained and reproducible from the data tables alone.

2.1 The sentiment pipeline

Our measurement of emotional flow was refined through three iterations, each prompted by a concrete failure of the previous approach.

First pass — manual readingVoyant Tools
We began by inspecting sentiment and counting tonal reversals by hand in Voyant. The scores were unreliable and the manual crossing counts were both slow and inconsistent. Resolution: abandon manual scoring.
Second pass — lexicon scoringVADER
A lexicon-based scorer (VADER) gave fast but coarse values that mis-read Early Modern diction. Resolution: move to a deep-learning sentiment model (siebert), and set LOWESS smoothing to a 150-unit window — finer and more legible than the 200-unit window used initially.
Third pass — zero-baseline problemDL · custom arc
Reading reversals against a fixed zero baseline was deficient: a play that lives entirely below zero registers almost no crossings, even when its tone visibly oscillates. Resolution: measure crossings against play-relative reference lines (start–end midline and overall mean), not against zero.

The resulting arc for each play is a deep-learning sentiment signal (siebert), LOWESS-smoothed at a 150-unit window and down-sampled to 150 points for display. From each arc we derive a compact statistical fingerprint, defined below, which is what the clustering in §3 actually operates on.

Crossing-count rule (team-defined)

A reversal is counted only where the smoothed arc crosses a reference line and remains on the far side for at least one full act. This suppresses spurious micro-oscillations of the smoother and keeps crossing counts comparable across plays of different length.

2.2 The network pipeline

For each play we built a co-occurrence network in which two characters are linked when they appear together in a scene, the edge weight being the frequency of that co-appearance. Before judging structure we fixed the visual encoding so that the same graphic grammar applies to every play:

Node colour= modularity class
A node's colour is its community, as detected by Gephi's modularity algorithm. Members of the same community share a colour, which makes factions, households, and camps legible at a glance and lets us see whether traffic between communities is heavy or sparse.
Node size= weighted degree
A node's diameter scales with its weighted degree — the summed weight of its scene-connections. Larger nodes are structurally central: they share the stage with many others, often repeatedly. Word-count (speaking volume) is retained as a secondary check, since centrality of presence and centrality of speech do not always coincide.
Edge thickness= weight
A thicker edge means the pair co-occurs more often. Thickness reports frequency of shared presence, not affection: a thick Iago–Othello tie marks how relentlessly the play keeps them on stage together, not how warmly.
Layout= ForceAtlas2 + NoOverlap
Force-directed placement draws strongly tied characters together; position is read as a hint only. Final judgements rest on the numbers (degree, density, path length, cross-community edges), not on where the layout happened to settle.

To keep the analysis focused on the dramatic core, nodes that sit visually far from the graph and carry a degree of one or less — walk-on parts with a single fleeting connection — were set aside before classification, so that centrality and density reflect the principal relational structure rather than incidental appearances.

§ 3 — The Emotional Axis

Five shapes a feeling can take

Hierarchical clustering on the arc fingerprints — mean polarity, emotional range, and the play-relative crossing counts — separates the thirty-seven plays into five emotional-arc types. Each is named for the gesture its curve makes. The instrument below is the working dashboard, re-implemented inside this report: select a type, choose a play, and its real smoothed arc is drawn; toggle a reference line to watch the counted crossings appear as markers. This is the interactive evidence behind the five types, not an illustration of them.

How to read the arc statistics

Four quantities do most of the discriminating work. Mean polarity places the play's overall tone above or below neutral. Range (peak minus trough) measures how much emotional distance the play traverses. Midline crossings — reversals around the line joining the opening and closing values — capture how often the trajectory changes its mind relative to its own beginning-to-end path. Mean crossings capture oscillation around the play's average mood. A high range with few crossings is a clean ascent or descent; a modest range with many crossings is restless but contained. These two behaving independently is what produces five distinct shapes rather than a single positive-to-negative spectrum.

The five types are The Pendulum (repeated swings across the emotional centre), The Ascendant (a trajectory that ends higher than it began), The Collapse (a wide, direct fall from peak to trough), The Steady (low oscillation and modest range), and The Outlier — a single statistically anomalous play, persistently negative with almost no oscillation, that refuses to join any group. These five labels become the first coordinate of every play's final type.

§ 4 — The Relational Axis

The architecture of who meets whom

Where the sentiment axis asks how a play feels, the network axis asks how it is built. The plate below renders one play's character network in full — Titus Andronicus, our worked example — using exactly the visual grammar fixed in §2.2. Drag a node to test the structure; hover to read a character's weighted degree and strongest ties. The Edge weight control thickens and brightens ties in proportion to how often the pair shares the stage, and prints the weight on the heaviest relationships, so the dramatic spine of the play stands out from its incidental contacts.

What the encodings mean — and why edge weight is foregrounded

Three readings should be available at a single glance, and the plate is designed to deliver them. Colour tells you the social geography: in Titus, the Roman court, the Andronici family, and the Gothic/peripheral faction each take a hue, so the play's central antagonism is visible before any name is read. Size tells you who anchors the structure: Titus, Lucius, Marcus, Aaron and Tamora swell to the largest nodes because their weighted degree is highest — they are repeatedly on stage with many others. Edge weight tells you the spine: the Marcus–Titus tie (weight 8) and Chiron–Demetrius tie (weight 6) are drawn thick and bright precisely because the play returns to those pairings again and again. Foregrounding weight matters because an unweighted graph would make a one-scene acquaintance look identical to a relationship the drama is built on; brightening and labelling the heavy edges restores that hierarchy.

4.1 Four structural questions

Each play's network was interrogated with four questions, each tied to specific metrics. Together they produce a fine-grained descriptor; §4.3 then explains how those descriptors were consolidated for the final typology.

Question 1 · Centre structure — one centre, two, or many?

Evidence: degree, word-count, graph position.

Ordered by concentration, a play is an Individual Network (one figure's degree and speech dominate), a Dual-Centre Network (two co-dominant figures), a Core Ensemble Network (three to five principals share centrality with a clear periphery), or an Ensemble Network (centrality broadly dispersed). Two community-led variants — the Community-Centred and Dual-Community networks — apply where a cluster, rather than a person, anchors the structure.

Question 2 · Network density — how tightly is the cast connected?

Evidence: density, average degree, average path length.

Density alone can mislead, so it is read together with average path length — the mean number of steps on the shortest route between any two characters. A short average path means almost everyone is reachable through a single intermediary; a long one means the cast is strung out across chains of acquaintance. The two metrics corroborate each other: high density with a short path length is a genuinely interwoven cast, while low density with a long path length is a dispersed one. We banded average path length as follows, and required density and path length to agree before assigning a density type:

For reference, the Titus plate sits at density 0.53 with an average path length of 1.49 — a very tightly connected, Dense Social Web, consistent with its compact and bloodily interlocked cast.

Question 3 · Cluster structure — are the communities joined or divided?

Evidence: modularity class, number of communities, cross-community edges among the top ties.

Because including every faint tie would let incidental encounters blur the play's real architecture, we ranked edges by weight and examined the top twenty relationships, counting how many of them cross community boundaries. To compare plays whose top-tie sets differ slightly in size after ties at the cut-off, the count is normalised to a twenty-edge basis: normalised cross-community edges = C / N × 20. The resulting bands run from Integrated Network (≥ 13 cross-community ties; communities thoroughly interwoven), through Interconnected Communities (8–12), Weakly Interconnected Clusters (4–7), to Divided Clusters (0–3; strong ties stay inside their own camp). A Divided Clusters with a Strong Bridge reading applies when an otherwise divided graph is held together by one decisive pair or broker, and a Dominant Core with Peripheral Clusters reading applies when one community monopolises the large nodes and heavy edges while satellites cling to it.

Question 4 · Edge pattern — spread across many ties, or concentrated on one?

Evidence: edge weights, gap between the first- and second-ranked ties, distribution of the top edges.

A Relational Web distributes strong ties across several pairs (the top edges are close in weight). A Relational Web with Core Dyads is broadly a web, but two or three relationships rise together before a sharp drop (e.g. weights 10, 10, 9, then 7, 6, …). A Weakly Focused Dyad has one clearly leading pair with other strong ties close behind, while a Focused Dyad has a single pair standing at least two weight-points above the rest. Because edge weight measures shared presence rather than intimacy, a heavy dyad marks dramatic centrality, not necessarily alliance.

4.3 Consolidation — from fine descriptors to working categories

The four-question descriptor is too granular to serve directly as a classification axis: used at full resolution it would split the canon into dozens of near-singleton types. Before building the final typology we therefore subsumed structurally equivalent descriptors into broader categories. This is not a discarding of analysis but a re-reading of it at the right scale — each broad category is defined by, and traceable back to, the detailed measurements.

Centre structure → Centre Profile

The centre descriptors collapse into three viewer-facing profiles. Single-Centre gathers the Individual and Community-Centred networks — one figure or one community is the reference point. Dual-Axis gathers the Dual-Centre and Dual-Community networks — two figures, camps, or relational poles share the structure. Ensemble-Based gathers the Core Ensemble and Ensemble networks — centrality is shared and the story cannot be reduced to one or two anchors. In the recommendation layer (§6) these become the immersion choices follow one figure, move between two poles, and track a whole ensemble.

Network density → recommendation granularity

Density is not used as a primary type boundary, because two plays with very different emotional shapes can share a density. Instead it is retained as a measure of relational load: Sparse networks are looser and easier to follow, Moderately Connected networks balance central and peripheral ties, and Dense Social networks demand attention to many simultaneous relationships. Density thus tells a viewer how much relational complexity to expect within a chosen type, rather than which type to choose.

Cluster structure → Social Field (the decisive axis)

The cluster descriptors carry the most classificatory weight, and they consolidate into two Social Fields. A Networked Field subsumes the Integrated, Interconnected, and Weakly-Interconnected readings: the communities are not sealed off, and emotional change can travel across the whole relational space. A Bounded Field subsumes the Divided, Bridged, and Dominant-Core readings: relational change is compressed inside a walled-off world, a single decisive bridge, or one dominant core. Social Field is chosen as the decisive structural axis because it is the broadest reading of relational space — it absorbs information from centre, density, and edge pattern alike, and answers the question that most changes the viewing experience: does feeling spread, or is it contained?

Edge pattern → descriptive support

Edge pattern is too technical to drive the top-level typology and is reserved as a supporting descriptor. At the consolidated scale it reduces to two families — a Relational-Web family, where strong ties are spread across several pairs, and a Focused-Dyad family, where one pair is comparatively sharper. It is used to characterise individual plays within a type, not to separate the types themselves.

§ 5 — The Composite Typology

Eight types, where feeling meets structure

The final classification crosses the emotional axis with the relational axis. The Sentiment Type names how the play's feeling moves; the Social Field names the relational space that movement travels through. Formally:

Final Type = Sentiment Type + Social Field

Five sentiment types and two social fields permit ten combinations in principle; two of them — Collapse + Bounded and Outlier + Bounded — have no occupants in the canon, leaving eight types. Each is named for a representative play and given a keyword label that captures its emotional and relational signature in the manner of the brief ("Slow Burn + Constellation").

§ 6 — Application

From eight types to one recommendation

The eight types answer "which kind of story would this viewer enjoy?" To answer "which title should they start with?", we layered a short funnel on top of the typology, reusing the same measured quantities as progressively finer filters rather than introducing new criteria.

Step 1 — TypeSentiment × Social Field
The viewer's emotional and relational preference selects one of the eight types — the broad family of plays.
Step 2 — ImmersionCentre Profile
Within a type, the consolidated centre structure offers a choice of follow one figure, move between two poles, or track a whole ensemble.
Step 3 — Relational loadNetwork density
Density translates into how much relational complexity to expect: looser, balanced, or densely interwoven.
Step 4 — CommitmentLength · cast size
Play length and number of speakers estimate the attention a title asks for: a short, a standard, or a long watch.

The result is a recommendation instrument that does not stop at "people who like this type like these plays," but lets the same viewer choose, inside a type, the immersion style, relational density, and time commitment that suit the moment. The instrument is delivered as a self-contained interactive built on the data tables in this report; no external service is required to run it.

Coda

Limits, honestly stated

Two caveats bound these results. First, automatic sentiment analysis of Early Modern English is imperfect: irony, archaic diction, and rhetorical inversion can all be mis-scored, so the arcs should be read as robust shapes rather than exact emotional readings — which is why the typology leans on the gross geometry of each curve (rise, fall, swing, hold) rather than on any single point. Second, Gephi supplies structure, not interpretation: betweenness, modularity, and density reveal that a bridge or a faction exists, but not what it means, so each metric was validated against well-known plays before being trusted on less familiar ones. Within those limits, the two-axis typology does what the brief asked — it sorts the canon by measurable properties of feeling and structure, names the resulting types, and maps each to an audience and an occasion that the wider library can inherit.
