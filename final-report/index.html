§ 1 · Overview

Why two axes

A classification cannot rest on impression, so we fixed in advance the two properties that most shape how a viewer bonds with a story.

Emotional flowSentiment axis
How feeling moves through the play over time — whether it rises, falls, swings back and forth, or holds steady. Measured from a deep-learning sentiment arc.
Character relationshipsNetwork axis
How the cast is wired — one protagonist, two camps, or a broad ensemble; one connected world or several walled-off ones. Measured from a character co-occurrence network.

These axes are independent: the same emotional shape feels very different depending on the relational space it plays out in. A steep fall through a tightly connected family (King Lear) is not the same experience as a steep fall scattered across isolated subplots. Keeping the two separate, and only combining them at the end, is what lets the typology capture distinctions a single genre label loses.

§ 2 · Sentiment Analysis

How a play feels, over time

We measure each play's emotional arc, then group the 37 arcs into five shapes. Getting a trustworthy arc took three iterations — each prompted by a concrete failure of the method before it.

The process: what broke, and how we fixed it

1
Manual reading Voyant Tools
Problem (before)

We read sentiment by hand in Voyant and counted tonal reversals manually. The scores were imprecise, which fed inaccurate crossing counts, and the whole process was slow.

Solution (after)

Abandon manual scoring entirely and move to an automated, reproducible sentiment pipeline so every play is scored the same way.

2
Lexicon scoring VADER → DL (siebert)
Problem (before)

VADER, a lexicon scorer, mis-read Early Modern diction and returned coarse values that did not track the real emotional contour of the verse.

Solution (after)

Switch to a deep-learning model (siebert) for sentence-level sentiment, and set LOWESS smoothing to a 150-unit window — finer and more legible than the 200-unit window we started with.

3
Baseline for counting crossings custom build on the professor's site
Problem (before)

Counting reversals against a fixed zero baseline was deficient: a play that lives entirely below zero shows almost no crossings even when its tone visibly oscillates.

Solution (after)

We built a new program on top of the professor's original site and count crossings against play-relative reference lines (the start–end midline and the overall mean) instead of zero.

Crossing-count rule (team-defined)

A reversal is counted only when the smoothed arc crosses a reference line and stays on the far side for at least one full act. This filters out spurious wobble from the smoother and keeps counts comparable across plays of different length.

The full scoring and arc-shape formulae are documented separately — see the Classification Formula page. The interactive dashboard below is the result: the final DL arcs for all 37 plays, grouped into the five emotional types.

Reading the five types

Two behaviours do most of the sorting: how far the arc travels (range) and how often it reverses (crossings). A wide range with few crossings is a clean rise or fall; a modest range with many crossings is restless but contained. Because these vary independently, the canon resolves into five shapes rather than one positive-to-negative line: The Pendulum (repeated swings), The Ascendant (ends higher than it began), The Collapse (a wide, direct fall), The Steady (low oscillation, modest range), and The Outlier (one statistically singular play). These five become the first half of every play's final type.

§ 3 · Character-Network Analysis

How the cast is wired

For every play we built a character network — characters are nodes, and two characters are linked when they share a scene, weighted by how often. We used DraCor for the corpus data and Gephi for the metrics and layout, then re-drew the graphs in-page from the exported JSON so you can explore any play.

What the graph shows you

Before judging structure we fixed one visual grammar for all 37 plays, so the same picture means the same thing everywhere:

Node colour= modularity community
A node's colour is the community Gephi's modularity algorithm puts it in. Same colour = same detected faction or household, so you can see at a glance whether the cast is one world or several, and how much traffic runs between groups.
Node size= weighted degree
A node's diameter scales with its weighted degree — the total weight of its scene-connections. Bigger nodes are structurally central: repeatedly on stage with many others. (Speaking volume / word-count was kept as a secondary check.)
Edge thickness= co-occurrence weight
A thicker, brighter edge means the pair appears together more often. It reports frequency of shared presence, not affection — a heavy Iago–Othello tie marks how relentlessly the play pairs them, not how warmly. The strongest ties are labelled with their weight.
Layout= ForceAtlas2 + NoOverlap
Force-directed placement (pre-computed in Gephi) pulls closely-tied characters together. Position is a hint; final judgements rest on the numbers, not on where the layout settled.

Characters sitting far from the graph with a degree of one or less — single-scene walk-ons — were set aside so the structure reflects the dramatic core.

3.1 · Four structural questions

Each play's network was read through four questions, each tied to specific metrics. Select a play in the explorer to see its answers and the underlying numbers in the side panel.

Q1 · Centre structure — one centre, two, or many?

Evidence: degree, word-count, graph position.

Ordered by concentration: Individual Network (one figure dominates) → Dual-Centre Network (two co-dominant figures) → Core Ensemble Network (3–5 principals with a clear periphery) → Ensemble Network (centrality broadly dispersed). Two community-led variants — Community-Centred and Dual-Community — apply where a cluster, not a person, anchors the structure.

Q2 · Network density — how tightly connected is the cast?

Evidence: density, average degree, average path length.

Density is read together with average path length — the mean number of steps on the shortest route between any two characters. A short path means almost everyone is reachable through a single intermediary; a long one means the cast is strung out in chains. We required the two to agree before assigning a density type, using these bands:

Q3 · Cluster structure — are the communities joined or divided?

Evidence: modularity class, number of communities, cross-community edges among the top ties.

Including every faint tie would let incidental meetings blur the real architecture, so we ranked edges by weight and examined the top twenty relationships, counting how many cross a community boundary. To compare plays whose top-tie set differs slightly after ties at the cut-off, the count is normalised to a twenty-edge basis: normalised cross-cluster = C / N × 20. Bands: Integrated Network (≥ 13), Interconnected Communities (8–12), Weakly Interconnected Clusters (4–7), Divided Clusters (0–3). Two special readings: Divided Clusters with a Strong Bridge (divided overall, but one pair/broker links the camps) and Dominant Core with Peripheral Clusters (one community holds the big nodes and heavy edges, satellites cling to it).

Q4 · Edge pattern — spread across many ties, or concentrated on one?

Evidence: edge weights, gap between the 1st- and 2nd-ranked ties.

Relational Web — strong ties spread across several pairs (top edges close in weight). Relational Web with Core Dyads — broadly a web, but 2–3 ties rise together before a sharp drop. Weakly Focused Dyad — one leading pair, others close behind. Focused Dyad — one pair at least two weight-points above the rest. Since weight is shared presence, a heavy dyad marks centrality, not necessarily alliance.

§ 4 · Consolidation

From fine descriptors to working categories

The four-question descriptor is too granular to be a classification axis on its own — at full resolution it splits the canon into near-singletons. So we subsumed equivalent descriptors into broader categories. This re-reads the analysis at the right scale; each broad category is still defined by the detailed measurements.

Centre structure → Centre Profile

Single-Centre (Individual + Community-Centred) · Dual-Axis (Dual-Centre + Dual-Community) · Ensemble-Based (Core Ensemble + Ensemble). In the recommender these become follow one figure, move between two poles, and track a whole ensemble.

Network density → recommendation granularity

Density is not a primary boundary (two plays with different emotional shapes can share a density). It is kept as a measure of relational load: Sparse (looser, easier to follow), Moderately Connected (balanced), Dense Social (many simultaneous relationships). It tells a viewer how much complexity to expect within a type.

Cluster structure → Social Field (the decisive axis)

The cluster descriptors carry the most weight and consolidate into two Social Fields. A Networked Field (Integrated + Interconnected + Weakly-Interconnected) lets emotional change travel across the whole relational space. A Bounded Field (Divided + Bridged + Dominant-Core) compresses change inside a walled-off world, a single bridge, or one dominant core. Social Field is the decisive structural axis because it is the broadest reading of relational space and answers the question that most changes the experience: does feeling spread, or is it contained?

Edge pattern → descriptive support

Too technical to drive the top-level typology, so it is kept as support — a Relational-Web family vs a Focused-Dyad family — used to characterise individual plays, not to separate the types.

Consolidated network criteria, at a glance

Centre Structure → Single-Centre · Dual-Axis · Ensemble-Based
Network Density → Sparse · Moderately Connected · Dense Social
Cluster Structure → Networked Field · Bounded Field (decisive)
Edge Pattern → Relational-Web family · Focused-Dyad family

§ 5 · The Eight Types

Where feeling meets structure

The final classification crosses the emotional axis with the relational axis.

Final Type  =  Sentiment Type  +  Social Field

Five sentiment types × two social fields gives ten possible combinations, but two — Collapse + Bounded and Outlier + Bounded — have no plays, leaving eight types. Each is named for a representative play and given a keyword label capturing its emotional + relational signature.

§ 6 · Application

From eight types to one title

The eight types answer "which kind of story?" To answer "which title first?", we layered a four-step funnel that re-uses the same measured quantities as finer filters.

Step 1 · TypeSentiment × Social Field
The viewer's emotional + relational preference selects one of the eight types — the broad family of plays.
Step 2 · ImmersionCentre Profile
Follow one figure, move between two poles, or track a whole ensemble.
Step 3 · Relational loadNetwork density
Looser, balanced, or densely interwoven — how much relationship-tracking the play asks for.
Step 4 · CommitmentLength · cast size
Short, standard, or long — the attention the title needs.

So the tool does not stop at "people who like this type like these plays": inside a type, the same viewer can pick the immersion style, relational density, and time commitment that suit the moment. Open the recommendation tool →

§ 7 · Limitations

Stated honestly

Two caveats bound the results. First, automatic sentiment analysis of Early Modern English is imperfect — both VADER and the DL model have known gaps on archaic diction, irony, and rhetorical inversion — so the arcs are read as robust shapes (rise, fall, swing, hold), not exact emotional readings. Second, Gephi gives structure, not interpretation: density, modularity, and betweenness show that a bridge or faction exists, not what it means, so each metric was validated against well-known plays before being trusted on less familiar ones. Within those limits, the two-axis typology does what the brief asked: it sorts the canon by measurable properties of feeling and structure, names the types, and maps each to a viewer and an occasion the wider library can inherit.
